# IT Operations Playbook

Good IT operations should largely be invisible.

People should be able to work, systems should be available, risks should be understood and technology should support the direction of the business.

When that happens consistently, IT has the opportunity to move beyond simply keeping the lights on and become a genuine business partner.

This repository brings together some of the principles and practices I use when thinking about how to operate, govern and continuously improve a technology function.

---

## My Approach

I tend to think about IT operations across six connected areas:

**Strategy → Governance → Delivery → Operations → Measurement → Improvement**

Each plays a different role.

**Strategy** determines where technology needs to go.

**Governance** makes priorities, ownership and risk visible.

**Delivery** turns decisions into change.

**Operations** keeps services reliable and secure.

**Measurement** tells us whether things are working.

**Improvement** prevents the operating model from becoming static.

The important part is keeping them connected.

---

## From Users to Customers

One of the mindset changes I value is moving away from thinking about the people IT supports simply as "users".

I prefer to think of them as **customers**.

A user consumes a system.

A customer has a business need, an expectation of service and a perspective on what is important.

That distinction influences how technology teams listen, communicate and prioritise.

---

## Business-Led Priorities

Technology teams will always have priorities that originate within IT:

- Cybersecurity
- Infrastructure lifecycle
- Technical debt
- Resilience
- End-of-life systems
- Regulatory requirements

But IT should not independently determine the relative business value of every technology initiative.

A healthier model combines:

**Business Value + Technology Risk + Cost + Capacity + Strategic Alignment**

This allows priorities to be discussed openly rather than existing inside an IT backlog that the rest of the organisation rarely sees.

## Service Management

IT service management provides useful structure around how technology services are delivered and supported.

That can include:

- Incidents
- Problems
- Requests
- Changes
- Service levels
- Knowledge
- Assets
- Configuration
- Continual improvement

The processes matter, but the objective should not be process for its own sake.

The more useful questions are:

**Are services reliable?**

**Can people get help when they need it?**

**Are recurring problems being fixed?**

**Can we introduce change without creating unnecessary disruption?**

---

## Resilience

Reliable operations also require preparation for when technology fails.

Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery therefore need to be connected to normal technology operations.

That means understanding:

- Critical services
- Business dependencies
- Recovery priorities
- RTO and RPO
- Backup and recovery
- Infrastructure dependencies
- Third-party dependencies
- Crisis and incident management

Most importantly, recovery needs to be tested.

A recovery plan that has never been exercised is still largely an assumption.

---

## Vendor Management

Modern technology functions depend heavily on external providers.

The objective is not necessarily to minimise the number of vendors.

It is to understand what capability each relationship provides and whether the overall supplier model remains effective.

Useful considerations include:

- Service performance
- Cost
- Risk
- Capability
- Security
- Contract obligations
- Concentration risk
- Strategic fit

Vendor management becomes particularly important when third parties operate critical technology services on behalf of the organisation.

## Technology Roadmaps

A roadmap should connect technology investment to business direction.

Rather than becoming a list of systems IT wants to replace, it should help explain:

- What capability needs to change
- Why the change matters
- What dependencies exist
- What risk is being addressed
- What investment is required
- What outcome is expected

A good roadmap allows technology conversations to become part of normal business planning.

---

## Executive Reporting

Technology reporting should help leaders make decisions.

Operational metrics are useful, but reporting becomes less valuable when executives are presented with large amounts of technical activity without context.

I prefer reporting that helps answer questions such as:

- Are critical services performing as expected?
- What material risks require attention?
- Are major initiatives on track?
- Where are we constrained?
- What decisions are required?
- Are we improving?
- What should leadership be concerned about?

The purpose of a dashboard is not to demonstrate how busy IT is.

It is to create visibility.

---

## Measuring What Matters

There is no shortage of things an IT function can measure.

The challenge is choosing measures that tell us something useful.

Depending on the organisation, that might include:

**Service** — availability, incidents, service levels and customer experience.

**Risk** — vulnerabilities, control effectiveness, resilience and material technology risks.

**Delivery** — roadmap progress, project outcomes and benefits.

**Financial** — budget, forecast, vendor spend and optimisation.

**Capacity** — demand, workload, capability and resource constraints.

**Improvement** — recurring problems removed, automation and process improvement.

Metrics should ultimately support a conversation or decision.

If nobody uses a metric, it is worth asking why it is being reported.

## Perspective

I don't see IT operations as the opposite of technology strategy.

Good operations create the foundation that allows strategy to happen.

If the organisation does not trust technology to provide reliable services, manage risk and deliver change effectively, it becomes much harder for technology to influence larger business decisions.

For me, the progression is:

**Reliable Service → Trust → Business Partnership → Strategic Influence**

That is where an IT function starts becoming a technology capability.
